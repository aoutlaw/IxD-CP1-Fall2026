# Reading Responses

Six quotes from each reading, with a short response to each.

---

## "What do Prototypes Prototype?" (Houde & Hill, 1997)

### 1. "Is a brick a prototype? The answer depends on how it is used."

This is the line that reframes the whole paper for me. It says a prototype isn't defined by what it's made of or how polished it is, but by the question it's answering. It also means almost anything on my desk can be a prototype if I'm clear about what I'm trying to learn from it.

### 2. "It is important to recognize that the degree of visual and behavioral refinement of a prototype does not necessarily correspond to the solidity of the design, or to a particular stage in the process."

This pushes back on the idea that design goes neatly from rough sketch to polished mockup. A glossy render can come early and a scrappy test can come late. I find this freeing, because it means I should choose the level of polish based on the question, not on how far along I'm "supposed" to be.

### 3. "Ironically, while the design team understood the meaning of the hand-drawn graphics, other members of the organization became enamored with the sketchy style to the extent that they considered using it in the final artifact."

This is funny, but it's also a warning. A prototype's style can become the message even when the designer meant it as a placeholder. It shows that people outside the team read prototypes literally, so the designer has to say up front what is and isn't being tested.

### 4. "One issue with prototypes like this one is that inexperienced audiences tend to believe them to be more functional than they are just by virtue of being shown on a computer screen."

This feels even more true today, when tools like Figma and AI generators can make something look finished in minutes. A screen gives a prototype authority it hasn't earned yet. Being honest about what's fake (the "smoke and mirrors") is part of the designer's job, not something to hide.

### 5. "The pizza box was a very efficient prototype. Spending virtually no time building it or considering options, the students got useful feedback on a basic design question—what physical form would be best for the user."

This is my favorite example in the paper. A weighted pizza box taught the team something that a beautiful CAD model never would have: that the form was wrong for how an architect actually carries things. It's a good reminder to test the cheapest version of an idea first, before I fall in love with the details.

### 6. "Implementation prototypes can be hard to build, and since they actually work, it is common for them to find their way directly into the final system."

This is the controversial one for me, because it's the opposite of the usual advice to "just ship it." Code written only to prove something works can end up as the foundation of a real product, along with all its shortcuts. The authors argue that even working prototypes should be treated as disposable, which takes discipline when something already runs.

## *Programming Design Systems*, Introduction (Rune Madsen)

### 1. "All in all, digital products all share a common trait: They are created with programming languages."

It's a simple statement, but it carries the whole argument of the book. If the medium is code, then a designer who can't touch code is always designing at a distance from the real thing. I felt this when building my responsive aquarium page, where many design decisions only made sense once I saw them in the browser.

### 2. "...static design tools like Illustrator and Sketch fail at prototyping digital systems."

This is a bold claim, and I think it's mostly right, but a bit too absolute. Static tools are great for quickly exploring look and feel, which Houde and Hill would call a valid prototype. Where they fail is in showing behavior, like how a layout reflows across screen sizes or how content changes over time.

### 3. "Throwing a design over the wall for production is a bad legacy of the printed page."

I like the image of "throwing it over the wall" because it captures how designers and developers often work separately. When the designer hands off a flat file, many decisions get made by someone who wasn't part of the design conversation. Learning to code lets the designer stay involved through the decisions that actually shape the experience.

### 4. "We use grid systems to balance our layouts and color circles to pick colors with proper distance to each other."

This is a smart way to make code feel less foreign to designers. We already follow rules and systems, we just apply them by hand. Writing those rules as code is a natural next step, and it lets one system produce many variations, like a layout that adapts to a phone and a desktop.

### 5. "...randomization can be used to reveal designs that the designer would never have created with a pencil."

This one challenges the idea that the designer must control every choice. Randomness can act like a collaborator that shows options outside my habits. The designer's role shifts from making each result to designing the system and then curating what it produces, which is a different kind of authorship.

### 6. "Is google.com a good website because of the look of the search field?"

This question is meant to provoke, and it works. Google's value is almost entirely in what happens after you type, not in how the box looks. It connects directly to Houde and Hill's "role" dimension: judging a digital product only by its look and feel misses the most important part of the design.

Three quotes from each of two readings on prototyping, with a short response to each.

“What do Prototypes Prototype?” (Houde & Hill, 1997)

Read it Here

“Is a brick a prototype? The answer depends on how it is used.”

This statement indicates that a prototype isn't defined by what it's made of or how polished it is, but by the question it's answering. It also means almost anything on my desk can be a prototype if I'm clear about what I'm trying to learn from it.

And I can see how this would be true because I have made prototypes using whatever is available at the time. There are very different ways to fulfil the same prototyping request.

“The pizza box was a very efficient prototype. Spending virtually no time building it or considering options, the students got useful feedback on a basic design question—what physical form would be best for the user.”

This is my favorite example in the story. A weighted pizza box taught the team something that the most perfect CAD model never would have: that the form was wrong for how an architect actually carries things. It's a good reminder to test a real-world version of an idea first before falling in love with the designed solution. For things that will end up in the physical world, if you truly want to prototype, it eventually needs to become a physical artifact.

“Implementation prototypes can be hard to build, and since they actually work, it is common for them to find their way directly into the final system.”

This is sooooo true! And I hate that it is. Before I left the company I ironically returned to and currently work at, I was teaching myself to write responsive code based on the Bootstrap framework. My test/prototype was successful, and I left the company with a nice single-page site with boilerplate content. After I left, they proceeded to use that proof-of-concept as the template to build out an entire $30 million per-year B2C site. Bad, bad, bad.

Programming Design Systems, Introduction (Rune Madsen)

Read it Here

“...static design tools like Illustrator and Sketch fail at prototyping digital systems.”

This was true when it was written, but it really doesn't hold up. Static tools are great for quickly exploring look and feel, which Houde and Hill would call a valid prototype. And to be fair, they are some of the first things that design students learn. But we're in a whole new world now where dynamic prototyping is commonplace practice for designers operating in digital spaces. It is an expectation that the prototype of any digital system will provide the ability for reviewers to interact with the proposed solution, or at least see that interaction emulated.

“Throwing a design over the wall for production is a bad legacy of the printed page.”

I totally agree with this, and have watched my own company overcome this behavior over the last 5 years. For designers who are used to operating that way, it's a challenging hurdle, but once they discover the freedom and control of being able to specify how their solutions should act and react, it's hard to deny them that opportunity. Even if the original request is, shall we say, challenging to develop.

“Is google.com a good website because of the look of the search field?”

Of course not. Google's value is almost entirely in what happens after you type, not in how the box looks. Judging a digital product only by its look and feel misses the most important part of the design. Amazon is another great example. Is it a sexy design? Not really. Am I going to buy a lot of shit from there because the site is extremely effective at its purpose? Yep!
